Assumptions and limits

What a link is, why it comes and goes, and how the what-if is built.

What a link is

A link from A to B means A's move one bar ago helps predict B's move now, on data the fit had not seen. It is a statistical lead, not proof that A causes B, and markets change: a link found in one stretch of history often disappears in the next. That is why each link is tested in several separate stretches, and why its steadiness is shown next to it.

What is deliberately left out

  • Pairs that only move together in the same bar have no time order, so they are listed apart from lead-lag links. In real daily data these are far steadier than leads and lags (gold miners with gold, airlines with the market), but the data cannot say which drives which; where both directions appear, the one into the more volatile series is ticked, the way a beta is read, and you can change it.
  • Stretches do not overlap, because overlapping stretches share bars and would count one chance correlation many times. The window says when they overlap.
  • Links with a delay of more than one bar may be missed, because the first screening looks at one-bar delays. For daily data this matters little.

How the what-if is built

  • Each series has a return per bar that follows the links you kept, and a running price change that adds it up. A bar is one unit of time.
  • A same-bar link is refitted against only the links you kept, so with one source it is that series' plain regression beta, and it acts in one step: a shock to one series reaches each linked series directly and never chains through another. Chaining conditional coefficients, which an earlier version did, made a fall in the S&P 500 move Brent up by 6%.
  • Every return fades towards zero: at the rate fitted from the data if one was found, and within about a bar otherwise. Without this a shock would carry on for ever.
  • A shock adds to a series' return for one bar. Its effect elsewhere follows only the links you kept: a same-bar link makes the follower respond within about a bar, by the size shown, and a lead-lag link about a bar later.
  • Checked against the recurrence fitted to the data, a shock's effect on a linked series came within a few percent of what the fitted link implies. The link itself is estimated, and estimates of a real effect tend to be a little smaller than the truth.

Replaying real moves, and the scorecard

  • Holding recent bars back lets the model learn up to an as-of date and then be tested against what happened next. The link analysis never sees the held-back bars.
  • A plain forecast of returns is close to flat, because returns are close to unpredictable, and would only look wrong beside a noisy real path. So the replay is conditional: the real moves of the series you choose are played through the model, and the others are compared with what they really did. The chosen series are held to their real path within a fraction of a bar.
  • Each followed series is scored against a guess of no change: was the model's number closer to what happened? Only series the model moved are scored. One or two comparisons are a data point, not proof.
  • Checked on five years of real Yahoo Finance data for seven series (gold, gold miners, airlines, Brent, banks, the S&P 500, the ten-year yield), replaying 21 bars from twelve different as-of dates: 26 comparisons, the model closer than a guess of no change 17 times (65%), and the direction right 17 times. Suggestive, not conclusive: the months overlap, and gold and gold miners were closer every time.
  • Each fitted link carries a constant, the average drift of the series in the learning window. The model leaves it out, so a series does not carry on drifting the way it happened to. Keeping it made the backtest worse.

The market guess, and the projection

  • The replay can also be scored against a market guess: each series moves by its usual beta to a market series (the S&P 500 when it is among your series), times what that market series really did. That is a harder test than no change, because much of what the links find is simply following the market.
  • On the real daily data above the model was closer than the market guess in 17 of 23 comparisons. On hourly data over about two years it was closer in 7 of 14, which is chance. Read that as: the links add something beyond a market beta on daily bars for a few pairs, and nothing shown on hourly bars.
  • Project forward runs the model with nothing shocked. The path is close to flat, because returns are close to unpredictable; the shaded ranges (68% and 95%) come from how much each series moved in your range, and are not the model's own uncertainty. Bars you held back are drawn against them, and the window counts how many series ended inside. About two in three should end inside the 68% range.

Limits

  • This is not a forecast, and it has not been validated against real markets or reviewed by a practitioner.
  • Only linear relationships between changes are modelled. Volatility, correlation breakdowns in a crisis and liquidity are not.
  • Prices in your files are taken as given: splits, dividends and stale quotes change the result and are not adjusted for.
